The Hawaii House Question

Can a ten-bedroom creator house in Hawaii pay for itself? Modelled across two islands, buy against lease, four revenue engines and every tax line the State actually charges. The short answer is that it can — but not as a house, and not on Oʻahu.

Verdict

Rent from influencers cannot carry this building in any configuration modelled. At $6,500 per creator per month, the cheapest version of the deal needs 104% occupancy and the most expensive needs 134%. There is no price at which creators fill the gap: covering costs on rent alone requires $107,000–$136,000 per creator per year.

One configuration clears a 12% hurdle. Hawaiʻi Island, purchased, with 30-day service terms, location days priced against the film credit and ten brand takeovers a year: 12.93% ten-year IRR, 2.78× debt coverage. Oʻahu is negative in every variant.

It is not an influencer house. It is a branded-content production venue that keeps a resident roster as inventory. Brand activations are 43% of revenue and every dollar of return sits on them.

And that 12.93% is the 82nd percentile, not the expected case. Section 08 rebuilds the analysis the way an underwriter would — with a pre-opening period, a monthly cash curve, correlated downside and 600 simulated paths. The median path loses money, the real capital requirement is $2,017,029 rather than $1,390,000, and there is a 27% chance of failing to cover debt service. Read that section before this one persuades you of anything.

Every assumption below is arguable, so open the live sandbox and change them — capacity, P&L, coverage, returns and funding need recompute as you move the sliders.

01 — What the law actually permits

Two statutes decide this before any spreadsheet does

Hawaii is the only state that taxes gross receipts on every rental, and one of the few that has spent the last four years actively legislating against short stays. Before modelling revenue, two legal facts fix the shape of the business.

The short-stay market is closed

Honolulu's Ordinance 22-7 set a 90-day minimum in residential districts. A federal court permanently enjoined the 90-day rule in December 2023, so enforcement runs on the older 30-day standard, and a second attempt in 2025 faces the same challenge. Either way, nothing under 30 days is available on Oʻahu outside resort zoning. Maui went further: Bill 9 became Ordinance 5909 in December 2025 and phases out roughly 6,200 apartment-district vacation rentals — West Maui by January 2029, South Maui by 2031. Litigation is filed; no injunction has issued.

So the model never assumes a nightly-rate business. Minimum term is 30 days, and the interesting question becomes where between 30 and 180 days you sit.

The film credit is real, generous — and excludes exactly what you were going to make

HRS §235-17 pays a refundable credit of 22% on Oʻahu and 27% on the neighbor islands. Act 185 (2026) adds five points for productions with at least 80% local hires, taking it to 27% and 32%. Qualified costs explicitly include "rentals and fees for use of local facilities and locations," "rentals of vehicles and lodging for cast and crew," and "airfare for flights to or from Hawaii." A house full of visiting creators is, line for line, a qualified expense.

HRS §235-17(o) — definitions

"Commercial" … does not include an advertising message with Internet-only distribution.

"Qualified production" … does not include … productions produced primarily for industrial, corporate, institutional, or other private purposes.

That is the sentence that kills the obvious plan. A TikTok or Instagram brand campaign shot at the house is statutorily excluded — internet-only distribution, produced for a private commercial purpose. You cannot build the business on claiming this credit.

Don't try to be the taxpayer. Be the qualified expense.

What survives is better anyway. Music videos, short films, streaming series, pilots, specials and TV-distributed commercials are qualified productions. When one of those rents the house, the production claims the credit on your invoice. You carry no compliance burden, file nothing, and your price becomes 22–32% cheaper to the customer without costing you a cent. It is a demand subsidy, and it prices as one.

Moving from Oʻahu to Hawaiʻi Island and hiring locally is worth 14.71% more gross revenue for an identical customer cost. The State pays the difference. One caveat sets the customer list: the production must spend $100,000 in Hawaii to claim anything, so single-day creator shoots never qualify. Multi-week series and music-video blocks do.

02 — The capacity ledger

A house is a fixed pool of bed-nights, and rent is the worst thing to sell

Ten beds is 3,650 bed-nights a year. Every revenue engine consumes them and nothing can be sold twice — which is the discipline most creator-house plans skip. Once you price each engine per bed-night consumed, the hierarchy is unambiguous.

Housing influencers is the least productive use of the asset — roughly a quarter of what a brand takeover yields on the same bed. And yet the sensitivity analysis says returns move more on the residency rate than on anything else, because residency occupies most of the calendar. The business is most exposed to its weakest engine.

Shifting dollars from a 70%-financed price to a 100%-equity renovation destroys more leverage than the subsidy returns. The OZ only works if the renovation itself is debt-financed.

05 — Where it breaks

Everything rests on brand activations

Eight sold takeovers a year holds a roughly 10% return. Five keeps the lights on. Below four the venture is underwater no matter what else goes right — the island, the tax structure and the film credit are all irrelevant if the brand pipeline does not exist.

Is $72,000 for a five-day takeover a real price?

Six resident creators producing three pieces each, at the low end of the published mid-tier rate card ($5,000 per post), is 18 assets worth $90,000 bought à la carte — before the hero film, the stills, and a location a brand cannot buy on Oʻahu. The price holds. The risk is not what you charge. It is whether eight to ten brands a year will book Hawaii at all, and no model can answer that. It is the first thing to test, before any property is committed.

06 — Entity and tax structure

Three decisions worth real money, and one genuine conflict

The 180-day line cuts both ways

This is the structural tension at the centre of the deal, and it is not obvious. Hawaii's Transient Accommodations Tax — 11% state from January 2026 plus 3% county — applies to any stay under 180 consecutive days. So 180-day leases save you 14 points on the lodging line.

But a 180-day lease is unambiguously a rental activity under Treasury Reg. §1.469-1T(e)(3), which makes your losses passive and traps the year-one bonus depreciation until you have passive income or you sell. Take 30-day terms and provide significant personal services — production support, programming, housekeeping, meals — and the §1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(B) exception applies: not a rental activity, material participation makes it non-passive, and the depreciation offsets other income.

If you own the building, take the 30-day service structure — $55,049 a year of TAT buys $389,400 of tax shield. If you lease it, there is no building to depreciate, the shield is small, and you should take the 180-day terms and skip TAT entirely. The right answer depends on whether you own, which is why this decision cannot be made before the tenure decision.

PropCo / OpCo, and Hawaii's pyramiding trap

Split title from operations: a Hawaii LLC holds the property, a second LLC — S-corp elected, to take reasonable salary and save self-employment tax on the rest — runs the business, holds the GET licence, employs staff and signs creator agreements. Standard, and it also lets you sell the asset and the business separately.

The trap is that Hawaii's GET has no general exemption for intercompany transactions. PropCo charging OpCo rent pays 4.5% on that rent while OpCo pays 4.5% on its revenue — you have taxed the same dollar twice for the privilege of good structure. The fix is HRS §237-16.5, which lets a lessee that subleases deduct 87.5% of the rent it pays:

$9,720

Per year, on $216,000 of rent. Pure pyramiding.

With the §237-16.5 deduction

$1,215

Same rent, 87.5% deducted. Saves $8,505 a year for a written sublease and a form.

Conditions: the sublease must be of the same real property, in writing, and PropCo must certify to OpCo that it is GET-licensed and taxable — without that certificate the deduction is presumed unavailable. File Form G-72. Note it covers the room sublease line only; activation and production service revenue pays full GET.

The rest of the stack

  • Cost segregation, always. With 100% bonus depreciation permanent under OBBBA, reclassifying ~25% of building basis plus 85% of the build-out gives $973,500 of year-one deduction on the Kona purchase. It is the single largest tax lever in the model — and it only pays if you have other income to shelter and you materially participate.
  • QBI §199A takes 20% off the federal rate on the operating income. A production-services and hospitality business is not a specified service trade, so it qualifies.
  • Property classification is a live risk. The model uses residential classification. Honolulu's Hotel/Resort rate is $13.90 per $1,000 against Residential A's $11.40, and Hawaiʻi County's is $11.55. Running a visibly commercial operation invites reclassification; budget for it.
  • HARPTA withholds 7.25% of gross sale price from non-resident sellers at exit. A cash-flow problem, not a tax problem, but it is real money tied up.
  • Watch the recapture. Cost segregation front-loads deductions into §1245 property, which recaptures at ordinary rates on sale — $389,400 in the base Kona exit. A 1031 exchange defers it; the OZ step-up eliminates it; doing nothing pays it.

Residency is 21% of revenue and 57% of the calendar. It is not the business — it is the inventory that makes the business sellable.

That inversion is the whole design. Brands do not buy houses; they buy access to creators and to a location. The resident roster is what makes a takeover worth $72,000, and the takeovers are what pay the mortgage. Price residency to fill beds and build the roster, not to cover costs — the model says it never will.

Why the collab-house era ended, and what this changes

Hype House peaked at 21 members with four actually living there, lost its top talent within months and was sold in 2024. The recorded failure mode is structural: content houses are built to incubate a collective, brands buy individuals, and the breakout stars leave. Any plan that monetises the talent inherits that failure.

This model monetises the place and the production capability instead. Talent churn becomes a scheduling problem rather than an existential one, because the asset — a permitted, equipped, credit-eligible production facility in a location nobody else can replicate — does not walk out.

What to test before committing capital

  • Sell four takeovers before you buy anything. Letters of intent from four brands at $72,000 is the whole thesis. Rent a house for one quarter and run them. If four will not sign, no property decision matters.
  • Confirm the production pipeline. Talk to Hawaii-registered production companies about location days at $6,618. They know the credit better than you do and will tell you fast whether the price is real.
  • Get the property class ruling in writing from the county before close, not after.
  • Price insurance for the actual use. The model carries $30,000; a commercial production and events rider on a coastal Hawaii property may not come in there.

Do those four things first and the model becomes a decision instead of a hypothesis. Skip them and the honest expected outcome is the base case on the previous page: a beautiful house, a negative NPV, and a lesson already learned publicly by everyone who tried it in Los Angeles.

08 — What a professional would change

Four holes in the analysis above, and what fixing them does

Everything to this point is a competent plan-level model. It is not what an institutional underwriter would accept, and the gaps all push the same way: they flatter the deal. Here is each one, fixed.

1. Revenue started in month one. It doesn't.

There was no pre-opening period — no closing, permitting, renovation, hiring or pre-selling. Nine months of that carries debt service, property tax, insurance, a founder and a manager, at $46,600 a month, before a dollar arrives.

2. "Equity in" was the wrong number entirely.

The figure quoted was down payment plus build-out. What you actually have to fund is the peak cumulative cash deficit — the trough of the monthly cash curve, which is deeper because the operating loss keeps digging after the pre-opening burn stops.

$2,017,029

3. The tornado moved one driver at a time. Reality doesn't.

A ±25% tornado is a diagnostic, not a downside case. If brands stop booking, creators stop coming too, retreats thin out and production days go with them — one demand factor moves all of them together. That is a different business:

A note on method — why these are NPV probabilities, not IRR

IRR is undefined whenever cash flows never change sign, which happens on precisely the worst paths. Ranking the simulation on IRR silently deleted 127 of 600 paths — all of them bad ones — and reported a healthier business than exists. NPV always exists. Every probability above is computed on NPV for that reason.

A correction to what I wrote earlier

Section 03 exited the property at appreciated value only, and I speculated in the working notes that a going-concern sale would be worth less. That was wrong. At a stabilized NOI of $413,624 growing with inflation, the business sells for $5.6M at a 10% cap against $3.5M for the house — so the appreciation-only exit was conservative by roughly 5 points of IRR.

That correction carries a second one worth more than the first. On weak paths the cap-rate value falls below the house value — and no rational seller takes $1M for a $3.5M house because the business underperformed. The residential value is a floor under the operating business: effectively a free put option, and in Hawaii, where residential prices are high relative to achievable NOI, an unusually valuable one.

That floor is the strongest argument for buying rather than leasing — and it is the one thing the lease case can never have.

It also converts the exit into a strategic instruction. The going-concern premium is only available if a buyer will underwrite the NOI, and today 43% of revenue is handshake brand deals that a buyer values at close to zero. Convert activations into multi-year contracts, because the contract is worth more at exit than the cash it produces while you hold it.

One sanity check that should have come first

$1,673,832 of revenue on ten beds is $167,383 per bed per year. A $600-a-night boutique hotel room at 70% occupancy grosses about $153,300 per key. So this plan asks a bed in a house to outperform top-tier boutique hospitality — possible only because activations and production days sell the same bed at four times the lodging rate. It is not absurd, but it is the kind of number to check before building ten sections on top of it, not after.

What an entrepreneur would do instead of any of this

A professional modeler answers "what is this worth?" An operator answers "what is the cheapest thing that could prove me wrong?" — and those produce different first moves. Everything above is downstream of one unknown: whether eight to ten brands a year will book Hawaii. No amount of modelling resolves it.

  • Rent a house for one quarter and run two activations. Roughly $60,000 to test the assumption that $2M of capital rests on.
  • Take a lease with a purchase option rather than choosing between buying and leasing. It buys the residential floor described above without committing the capital, and the option itself has value the model above never priced.
  • Sell the calendar before buying the building. Four signed LOIs change the financing conversation completely, and they are also exactly what makes the going-concern exit real.
  • Write the kill criteria down first. "Fewer than four signed activations by month nine" is a decision. "It's still early" is not.

The model's real output is not 12.93%. It is a list of the four numbers worth spending money to learn, ranked — and the observation that three of them can be tested for less than 3% of the capital at risk.
